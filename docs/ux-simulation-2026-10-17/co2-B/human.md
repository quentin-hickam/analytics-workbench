# Feedback from Sam Whitfield

## 1. Outcome and satisfaction

I got what I needed from this workbench: specific answers to all five questions, an eight-slide main storyline with three appendix slides, chart data, three supporting tables, and a short donor methodology note. I reviewed the draft, requested corrections, and accepted release 001. I opened START-HERE.md in the external releases folder. I have not built or inspected the actual PowerPoint, so my acceptance covers the package rather than finished slides.

Satisfaction: **8/10**. I would use this again, particularly for analysis that needs a clear record and repeatable delivery. I would want less procedural chatter and more reliable qualifications in the first results report.

## 2. Top pain points, ranked

1. **Historical missing data was initially described as zero — major.** In the first answers report, the agent said China's and India's missing early years counted as zero. After I challenged that, it corrected the description to sums of reported years. It then called an assumption-based sensitivity calculation a ceiling, which needed another correction. Missingness should have been checked and explained before presenting historical responsibility figures. A scenario should arrive with its assumption and without an unsupported claim that it bounds the truth.
2. **Review became an extended editorial approval process — major.** After the full draft, the agent stopped with seven wording problems following a second review. Some were useful; deciding whether “cut” implies deliberate action felt disproportionate. Further repairs and reviews followed, and adding the final review rule triggered another review at release. Resolve routine wording consistently before asking me to approve headlines, and reserve decisions for changes to meaning or material caveats.
3. **Imprecise wording changed the apparent meaning — major.** The preview described consumption accounting as counting imports, without exports. The draft described a rising coal share of CO2 as a change in the energy mix. Both needed my correction. These are distinctions the analyst should catch before donor review.
4. **Useful answers were buried in internal narration — minor.** Replies included running commentary about producers, settings, mechanical checks, rule warnings and review procedures. “That producer came out convoluted” and the spurious warning about a number were not useful to me. Lead with results, decisions and practical limitations; summarize the machinery in one sentence.
5. **Initial ranking answers were incomplete — minor.** The first report named the top ten but supplied values for only a few. Coal eligibility was also ambiguous until I asked: 60 eligible, 59 analyzed. Give complete requested rankings, units and denominators on the first pass, even if the tables are attached separately.

None of these ultimately blocked delivery.

## 3. README

The quick-start example was useful: it let me supply the question, audience, deadline, storage locations and desired package together. It clearly explained that the workbench produces a deck package rather than a .pptx, that headlines can be approved first, and that release freezes a numbered copy. Those expectations proved accurate.

The document is long for a first-time analyst, especially once installation and host-specific material begin. A shorter analyst-only entry point would help. The glossary was useful, but I still had to absorb more distinctions between provisional answers, approval, review and checks than I wanted.

The README warned that review could stop after two passes, so that interruption was not undocumented. What it did not make tangible was how small an issue could trigger that stop, or that adding a review rule immediately before delivery could trigger another review. A concrete example distinguishing a substantive user decision from an editorial repair would help.

It also did not tell me how to interpret an “unassessed join check,” which the agent surfaced during analysis. I needed the practical consequence, not more helper documentation. The README correctly says checks do not redo the analysis; my experience reinforced that I still needed to challenge missing-data assumptions and accounting language myself.

## 4. Communication and scoping

The four initial method questions were strong. Each had a recommendation and an understandable alternative, including a clear percentage-point example. They addressed real choices: endpoints, minimum changes, the world denominator and the meaning of fastest coal-share decline.

Announcing a second scoping round for simple choices risked slowing setup. I supplied end-year ranking, the consumption scope and my expectations in the next reply, which avoided that extra exchange. Recommendations could have covered those choices in the original round where possible.

The substantive reports were generally readable. The process commentary was too long and sometimes jargon-heavy: producers, result settings, mechanical checks, promotion and join checks. I appreciated plain summaries of what changed and what I needed to decide. I did not need repeated Git reminders or details of each repair pass.

## 5. Trust in the answers

- **Territorial increases and decreases:** I accepted these after receiving complete baseline, endpoint and change tables. Explicitly handling Kosovo, Antarctica and aggregates helped. The rounding explanation resolved apparent subtraction inconsistencies. I did not independently recalculate the dataset.
- **Lower emissions with higher GDP:** I trusted the qualified count of 45 more than an unqualified claim of successful decoupling. The agent made the 2022 GDP limit, the 164-country coverage and seven declines under 5% visible. That matters especially for Australia's 0.3% decline.
- **Consumption comparison:** This was particularly useful and credible because both measures used 2023. I accepted the figures once the explanation covered imports and exports. Comparing different endpoint years would have weakened it.
- **Cumulative shares:** This was the answer I trusted least initially. I accepted the final figures as shares of reported emissions, with the historical gaps visible. The illustrative scenario provides context; it does not establish the missing emissions or a hard upper bound.
- **Coal-share changes:** I accepted the final ranking after the 60-eligible/59-analyzed distinction and missing Libya data were clear. I trust it as a change in coal's share of CO2, not as a measure of the energy mix or program effectiveness.

I also appreciated that the agent left Ukraine's explanation untested rather than turning a plausible explanation into a finding. None of these answers establishes which countries will welcome or benefit most from our program; the final implications correctly preserve that distinction.

## 6. Package review and release

I read findings.md and the donor methodology note, then inspected the revised passages and START-HERE.md. The outline was coherent and the methods note usable. Eight main slides plus an appendix was a reasonable solution to the length request. I accepted the 541-word note as approximately one page.

Review caught worthwhile problems, including an unsupported assurance that limitations did not affect rankings and an overstatement about historical shares. That increased my confidence. The repeated editorial stops reduced the benefit somewhat.

START-HERE clearly separated donor attachments, builder inputs and internal files. Release 001 was copied to the requested storage location, and I could open its instructions there. The agent clearly explained that later changes would become release 002 and respected my request for no Git commit. I did not audit every released file or verify the actual rendered deck.

## 7. Wasted exchanges or effort

The largest avoidable effort was correcting the early-gap interpretation, then correcting the ceiling language, followed by several rounds of wording review. Giving complete ranking tables initially would also have removed a follow-up request. Routine “cut” versus “fell” consistency could have been settled before headline approval.

The status request, headline checkpoint and final release approval were useful exchanges. I cannot assess elapsed runtime from this transcript; my frustration was with repeated gates and lengthy narration rather than a measured wait.

## 8. What worked well

- The agent carried five distinct questions through to a concrete, released package.
- Definitions and comparison years were explicit, with matched years for the consumption comparison.
- Country classification and coverage checks exposed meaningful exceptions instead of silently dropping them.
- Small declines and historical gaps remained visible in the final caveats.
- The agent accepted corrections, carried them into the methods and saved review rules for future work.
- Supporting datasets made the top-ten slides useful without making the main deck unwieldy.
- The final handoff and external release path were clear enough for me to continue with a deck builder.

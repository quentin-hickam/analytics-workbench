# Feedback from Sam Whitfield

## 1. Outcome and satisfaction

I got a useful answer to all five questions, an eight-slide main deck outline with three appendix slides, a donor-facing methodology note, chart files and three accompanying tables. Release 001 exists in both the project and the specified releases folder; I checked that both folders contain the package files. I accepted the historical answer specifically as shares of recorded emissions, with missing years disclosed.

Satisfaction: **7/10**. I would use this again for a similar research briefing, but I would budget for substantive human review. I would not send its first “checked” draft directly to donors. It helps prepare a country-selection discussion; it does not supply the policy-opportunity or partner evidence needed to make that decision.

## 2. Ranked pain points

1. **Historical missing data surfaced late — major.** After the full draft had passed checks and review, the agent introduced a caveat that missing early China and India values “count as zero.” When challenged, it acknowledged they were unknown. It then inferred that the World total excluded those emissions because country totals reconciled, and I had to challenge that too. This was the biggest trust problem. Historical coverage and the treatment of missing values should have been assessed before the first cumulative-share finding, with documented source facts distinguished from inference.

2. **Review loops kept moving the finish line — major.** Full-draft preparation and nearly every revision produced several “final” review passes. Even after I requested only two final changes, unrelated wording changed again. Review caught real problems, but a clean review never felt durable. A consolidated review before showing me a draft, followed by a concise account of consequential changes, would have helped. I cannot quantify elapsed time from this exchange, but the repeated progress messages and approval rounds made the process feel long.

3. **Review rules sometimes made the presentation worse — major.** The subtitle eventually became a list of every comparison period because a reviewer wanted all dates named. The Answer caveat accumulated many distinct qualifications in one bullet. A technical reconciliation tolerance initially led the methods appendix. I had to restore a short subtitle and move the tolerance into notes. Review needs to assess readability as well as completeness, with dates attached to the relevant claims rather than crammed into every location.

4. **Initial answers were incomplete — minor.** The first Q1 answer named 20 countries but gave changes for only three. The Q2 list mostly omitted figures, and the extra consumption check said “most” without a count. I needed another exchange for complete rankings and denominators. A compact results table at first answer would have saved that round.

5. **Repeated version-history reminders — minor.** The agent repeatedly said nothing had been committed and invited me to request a commit, despite the README saying it would remind me at most once. Explain this once, then let me proceed unless it actually prevents delivery.

6. **One presentation instruction took two attempts — minor.** I asked to keep the numerical reconciliation tolerance in speaker notes. The next response still put it in a visible methods bullet. It was corrected after I repeated the request. A final comparison against my explicit edits would have helped.

## 3. README

The README correctly explained that I would receive a package for building slides, not a PowerPoint file. The first-message example, storage guidance, headline checkpoint, status request and numbered-release explanation were useful. Providing the storage paths up front worked: I did not have to repeat them.

It is long for a research lead, even though it says installation material can be skipped. I would prefer a short analyst path with administration in a separate document.

The warning that checks do not redo the analysis was accurate and important. What I needed more explicitly was that “all questions answered” and “clean independent review” can still coexist with an undiscovered source-coverage assumption. A short example involving missing values or unsupported interpretation would make that limitation concrete.

I also needed a clearer expectation for repeated reviews after small edits: what can reopen already approved text, and how additional changes will be presented. The README explains review rules but did not prepare me for the amount of revision churn. Its once-per-session commit-reminder promise was not followed. The advertised exchange range roughly fitted our nine user messages including closure; I have no reliable elapsed-time comparison.

## 4. Communication and scoping

The initial interview was good. Recommendations and alternatives made single-year endpoints, eligibility thresholds, historical EU membership and the global denominator easy to decide. Asking what the program actually does was appropriate. The fourth question bundled several choices, but they remained understandable. The agent verified initially tentative coverage years after downloading the data.

Most terminology was explained well. I could work with territorial versus consumption accounting, percentage points and the difference between observation and explanation. I did not need operational details such as validation settings, glossary exports or repeated internal-review narration. “File fingerprints” and version-history reminders were peripheral to my immediate decision.

Messages became too long near completion. I wanted changed passages and material warnings; I often received a full recap and another release invitation. Short status endings were useful. The agent was candid when challenged and showed extra edits rather than silently changing approved text.

## 5. Trust in the answers

- **Q1:** I accepted the complete quantitative rankings after the follow-up. Explicit country filtering, endpoint years and the Ukraine caveat helped. I requested wording that made clear these were changes in annual emissions, not cumulative additions.
- **Q2:** I accepted the 45-of-164 result and largest-country table after the agent supplied the figures and explained that any decrease qualified. The near-flat cases and GDP exclusions were important. This supports a descriptive comparison, not a claim that growth caused reductions.
- **Q3:** I trusted this most readily: all four entities used the same 2023 comparison year, with both per-person values displayed. The initial headline alone would not have covered all four, but the draft did.
- **Q4:** This had the weakest initial basis. I did not accept missing values as zero or the unsupported assertion about the World series. I accepted the final percentages only with the recorded-emissions qualification and explicit missing years. Complete historical shares remain beyond what the agent established from this dataset.
- **Q5:** I accepted the ranking once the 50 Mt threshold, percentage-point definition, endpoint years and Libya exclusion were explicit. A falling coal share is not by itself evidence of a particular policy succeeding.
- **Additional consumption check:** Useful and relevant, but “most” and then “27 of 45” needed refinement to 27 of 39 with data, with six missing. I accepted it as a test of whether cuts also appear under another accounting basis, not an explanation of imports or offshoring.

I did not independently recompute these results. My confidence rests on the reported checks, specific tables, source definitions and review of the narratives. Reconciliation supports internal consistency; it cannot establish completeness or source construction.

## 6. Package review and release

The headline preview was useful. I approved eight main slides, methods and limitations in the appendix, and three supporting tables. I opened findings.md, methodology-note.md and START-HERE.md and could understand what to review and what to send to donors or the builder.

The package incorporated my meaningful corrections: annual-emissions wording, a better consumption denominator, all four per-person comparisons, explicit ranking eligibility and a note that country screening is not partner selection. The independent review also caught misleading causal implications, which was valuable despite the churn.

Release itself was clear: explicit approval, release 001, frozen and copied paths, and a list of caveats. I verified the package files were present in both locations; the agent reported that the copies matched. I have not built or inspected an actual PowerPoint, so I cannot assess the rendered charts or the Microsoft 365 handoff. START-HERE.md makes that next step understandable.

## 7. Wasted turns or time

The biggest avoidable exchanges were completing the initial numeric answers, correcting the missing-as-zero historical assumption, removing the unsupported World-series claim, and simplifying the expanded subtitle. Moving the tolerance required a repeated instruction. Repeated “final” reviews and commit reminders added noise. Some review time was clearly justified by genuine errors, but those errors should have been consolidated before asking me to approve a clean draft.

## 8. What worked well

Setup and data acquisition proceeded without unnecessary permission questions. Storage locations persisted. Recommendations made scoping efficient, and the country/aggregate distinction was explicitly checked. The status report was easy to understand. Different coverage years, small qualifying reductions, and missing consumption data were made visible. The additional consumption comparison helped answer the underlying research question without expanding into an unrelated investigation.

The final package is useful, documented and easy to hand off. The agent accepted corrections and ultimately removed unsupported claims. The workflow's strongest feature is keeping the answers, chart data, methods and release together; its biggest improvement opportunity is catching substantive source assumptions earlier and making review converge.

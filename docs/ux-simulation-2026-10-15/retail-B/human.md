# Feedback from Priya Natarajan

## 1. Outcome, satisfaction and reuse

I got specific answers to all five questions, supporting tables I could inspect, a methodology note, and an approved deck package frozen as release 001 in our releases folder. I did not get a finished PowerPoint presentation: assembly still needs another app. That distinction matters with a leadership meeting approaching.

Satisfaction: **7/10**. I would use it again for analysis with an audit trail, provided it reduces the approval loops and gives me a clearer route to a finished presentation. I had to act as a fairly active editor of the evidence and language.

## 2. Ranked pain points

1. **Repeated permission to do work already requested — major.** After the first findings, the agent asked whether to draft the deck. After I explicitly requested awb-package, it still stopped for headline approval before writing anything. A provisional storyline followed by the full draft would have helped; reserve approval for definitions that change the numbers and final release.
2. **Interpretation ran ahead of evidence — major.** The first analytical response suggested April's fall was probably calendar timing, called some cancellations product problems, and described growth across widening customer bases without showing all the counts. It also included unchecked quick queries. I needed all presentation figures checked before being presented as findings, with observations separated from hypotheses and recommendations.
3. **Useful presentation elements arrived only after prompting — major.** The initial draft had no overseas growth chart and the cancellation slide put the leading product names in notes rather than the visible slide. The supposedly full reconciliation was a paragraph of main differences. A business audience needs named products, a visible country ranking and a reconciliation that actually closes. These should have been in the first draft.
4. **Technical reporting overwhelmed the business conversation — minor.** The setup response covered environments, helper scripts, Git, server registration and configuration. Later responses described internal checks and exceptions. I needed progress, decisions and review links, with technical material available separately.
5. **Package mechanics appeared to dictate the story — minor.** The agent said changing slide order meant changing question priorities, and initially explained the missing charts through a chart-file restriction. These constraints were confusing and did not help my decision. It should accommodate a sensible leadership narrative without involving me in internal bookkeeping.
6. **The final mile requires another tool — major for meeting preparation.** Release contained an outline, data and assembly instructions, rather than a rendered deck I could open and inspect. The README disclosed this, but the work and access needed for the handoff deserve much more prominence.
7. **Presentation formatting needed another correction — minor.** The revised product-table preview showed cancellation rates as decimal fractions. Leadership-facing percentages should be formatted automatically; I should not need to request 95.1% instead of 0.9513.

No issue blocked the simulation from completing.

## 3. README

The opening examples for awb-init, awb-status, awb-package and awb-release were useful. The README correctly explains that drafting and release are separate and that Microsoft 365 assembles the presentation.

For a first-time business user, however, installation, host configuration and implementation detail dominate the document. I wanted a short business-user route: what to ask, what decisions I will need to make, what files I will receive, what to review and what constitutes delivery. Put the technical setup in a separate section or document.

I needed a prominent statement near the beginning that a deck package is an outline plus data and instructions, not a PowerPoint file, and an explanation of required access to the assembly app. A small example package or illustrated handoff would make this clearer.

The README did not prepare me for a separate headline-approval gate, extra reference slides being treated as required, slide order apparently depending on question priorities, or chart-file constraints being offered as reasons to omit useful visuals. I could not tell whether these were product rules or the agent's choices. Clarify what is required and what is flexible.

It should also explain in plain language whether an accepted limitation such as unknown VAT treatment can remain open at release. The agent eventually said nothing was outstanding while also saying VAT was still open. I accepted delivery with that caveat; I did not consider the VAT question answered.

## 4. Communication and scoping

The agent asked useful commercial questions: merchandise versus all recorded lines, cancellation timing, minimum product sales and growth ranking. Recommendations made them easy to answer. The distinction between confirmed sheet overlap and possible repeated invoice entries was important.

It should have bundled all six definition decisions into one concise interview instead of announcing another round. I supplied both concentration denominators to resolve ambiguity. Asking me to approve commits, confirm VAT again or re-authorise drafting added no value.

Messages were usually too long for a busy head of e-commerce. Terms such as canonical views, validated results, manifests, helper environments and review exceptions belong behind the scenes. The status response was much better: it explained what was complete, what was open and what remained.

Commercial interpretation was useful when qualified. I appreciated the eventual clear statement that these data support priorities, not a precise budget split or a forecast of marketing returns.

## 5. Trust in the answers

I accepted all five final answers, with the agreed definitions and caveats. Trust came from being able to read all 24 monthly rows, all 12 cohort rows, named products and country rankings, rather than just hearing that checks passed.

- **Revenue and seasonality:** I trusted the final monthly table and year totals, backed by the closing reconciliation and removal of confirmed sheet overlap. I did not trust the initial Easter explanation, which was untested and removed.
- **Customer concentration:** I trusted the final 54.8% of all net merchandise revenue and 63.2% of identified-customer revenue because the denominators became explicit. Missing-ID revenue was reported separately. Lack of an ID in this export does not establish what customer information exists elsewhere.
- **Repeat buying:** I accepted 40.5% as a result for first observed customers, with later-day purchases within 90 days. I would not present the cohort differences as deterioration in genuinely new-customer retention, given the limited earlier history.
- **Cancellations:** I trusted the final rankings and values after seeing gross sales, cancelled value and invoice counts. The distinction between one large cancellation and many separate cancellations helped. I did not accept claims about stock, quality or delivery causes; those need investigation outside this export.
- **Overseas growth:** I trusted the pounds ranking and both-year customer counts. Australia visibly leading the ranking matters. France and Germany being acquisition-test candidates is a commercial judgement, not proof of the best return on spend. More identified customers does not prove acquisition.

I did not audit code or independently recompute the analysis. My acceptance rests on the reviewable outputs, consistency and explanations. Automated checks and independent review support that trust but do not replace it.

## 6. Package review and release

The outline and CSVs were accessible and useful. Revisions were responsive: the agent added the six-product table, the top-seven overseas chart and complete reconciliation. I reviewed the revised files before approving delivery.

The first independent review reportedly took eight rounds. That was surprising, though the resulting corrections were worthwhile. I need to hear material changes and remaining uncertainty, not the machinery of every review cycle.

Release itself was clear and reassuring: release 001, a final storage location, confirmation that the copied files matched, and an explanation that later changes would require another release. That part worked well. I still cannot judge the final slide layout or chart legibility until PowerPoint assembly happens.

## 7. Wasted turns and time

The main avoidable delays were asking whether to draft after I had already requested it, then stopping again for headline approval. A full draft could have arrived at least one exchange earlier. Technical setup explanations, repeated caveat confirmation and optional server configuration added reading without helping me advance the decision.

The first findings should have included the requested monthly and cohort detail, or links to those tables. The first draft should have contained a country growth chart and visible product names. The percentage-formatting correction was also avoidable.

Definition agreement, checking the draft and approving release were worthwhile uses of my time. I did not observe elapsed processing time closely enough to assess actual runtime.

## 8. What worked well

The agent handled data acquisition and storage, detected the overlapping sheets, and explained a serious double-counting risk clearly. It accepted my decision to retain ambiguous repeated lines and quantified the sensitivity instead of silently deleting them.

The agreed definitions remained visible. Corrections to first-observed-customer language, customer-share denominators and cancellation interpretation carried through later drafts. The final supporting tables made all five questions answerable rather than leaving me with vague headlines.

The combination of commercial findings, methodology, chart data and frozen external release is valuable. With fewer checkpoints, tighter language and a clearer presentation handoff, this could become a practical repeatable workflow for my team.

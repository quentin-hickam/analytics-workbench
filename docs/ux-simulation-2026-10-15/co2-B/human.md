# Feedback from Sam Whitfield

## 1. Outcome and satisfaction

Release 001 was frozen and copied to my releases folder, with all 19 files reported to match. I accepted the answers with their documented caveats.

## 2. Ranked pain points

1. **Validation confidence exceeded what had actually been checked — major.** During draft review, I questioned the statement that country totals plus all non-country entries reconciled to World. The agent later acknowledged that it had checked only six sample years. Checking every year revealed a 478 Mt double count in 1991 from the Kuwaiti oil fires. Rankings and shares were unaffected, but repeated “all checks pass” and clean reviewer reports had made the claim sound settled. What would help: state the scope of each check, test the full period before making a full-period claim, and have reviewers challenge the substance of reconciliation and missing-data claims.

2. **Repeated gates before I could see a draft — major.** After the first results I was asked to confirm selection rules and authorize a commit. After asking for a draft, I got proposed headlines and another approval request, with no draft files. What would help: batch consequential choices early, proceed on routine bookkeeping, and give me a concrete draft with provisional editorial choices marked. If headline approval is mandatory, tell me that at the start.

3. **Missing historical records were minimized without support — major.** The first draft treated absent years as adding zero and said the effect mattered little because early emissions were small. The revised package disclosed 57 missing years for China and 19 for India and supplied conservative bounds. What would help: make coverage a standard part of cumulative-share reporting, distinguish missing from measured zero, and avoid an assurance about materiality until it has been assessed.

4. **The package structure expanded my slide count — minor.** At headline approval, the agent said required sections brought my roughly eight-slide request to twelve. Eight core slides plus an appendix worked, but appendix marking initially appeared only in speaker notes. What would help: agree on core versus appendix at scoping and mark it visibly in builder instructions and headings.

5. **Technical detail crowded out the research conversation — minor.** The first setup report described Python packages, an alternative installer, Git, helper files and optional server registration. Later I was pointed to SQL to review classification. What would help: summarize readiness and any practical limitation; put technical details in records and give me plain-language review material.

6. **The country screen introduced discretionary thresholds late — minor.** The agent first counted appearances in rankings, then introduced a five-percentage-point coal threshold and an additional rationale for India. What would help: describe this as an illustrative organizing device from the outset, show the supporting evidence, and avoid implying the thresholds identify program impact.

## 3. README

The example requests for initialization, status, packaging and release were useful. The README also correctly described frozen releases, external storage and the Microsoft 365 assembly step.

For a first-time research user, it is too long and mixes installation, host configuration and everyday use. I needed a short “installed already: start here” path. I also needed an early, prominent explanation that the delivered deck package is an outline and data files, not a rendered slide deck.

The mandatory headline-approval stop and the apparent requirement for four extra sections were surprises. The README mentions checking the storyline but does not clearly explain the approval gate or its effect on slide count. It should explain which decisions I must make and which the agent can handle by default.

“Validation” and “review” need clearer practical limits. I needed to know whether a clean review meant editorial compliance, numerical reproduction, source-definition verification, or full coverage checks. In this session it did not prevent an incorrect full-period reconciliation claim.

I found no clear factual error in the README itself. The largest gaps were expectation-setting and the absence of a concise nontechnical workflow.

## 4. Communication and scoping

The initial four questions were sensible, with helpful recommendations: evidence versus recommendation, endpoint comparisons, country definition and a common consumption year. Scoping correctly considered the audience and what emissions data cannot say about program opportunity.

However, the agent deferred several method questions to another round even though they could have been bundled. Reporting 15 open questions, many answerable from the data, made the project feel more bureaucratic than it needed to be. I supplied the remaining method choices together to keep things moving.

The first setup reply was much too long for my role. Terms such as canonical views, publication, result linkage, ledger IDs and interpreter setup belong in the audit trail. The numerical answer summary was much better: short, specific and organized around my questions. Later replies improved when I asked for findings and decisions rather than implementation detail.

## 5. Trust in the answers

- **Q1, absolute changes:** I accepted the country rankings and magnitudes. Explicit endpoints, aggregate exclusions, full supporting rankings and Russia/Ukraine caveats helped. I did not independently recompute the values.
- **Q2, CO2 down and GDP up:** I accepted 45 of 164 countries for 2005–2022. The denominator, GDP coverage limit and disclosure of marginal reductions made the answer credible. It should not be read as proof of sustained or policy-caused decoupling.
- **Q3, consumption versus territory:** I accepted the shared 2023 comparison after the wording was corrected to net emissions embodied in trade. The initial import wording was loose, and rounded endpoints needed an explanation for differences that did not visibly subtract.
- **Q4, cumulative shares:** This needed the most scrutiny. I accepted the final values as shares based on the available records, with missing-year coverage and conservative bounds disclosed. I did not trust the original unsupported assertion that missing early years were immaterial.
- **Q5, coal share:** I accepted the percentage-point ranking, with Libya excluded transparently. The explanation that a falling share can reflect changes in coal or other sources was useful; it avoids treating a mix change as a causal policy result.

I accepted the further-assessment list as an illustrative screen, not a validated priority ranking. Exact supporting rankings improved transparency, but policy opportunity and our ability to act still require separate evidence.

## 6. Package review and release

I reviewed the actual findings outline and methodology, then checked revised historical coverage tables and relevant narrative passages. That review was productive: visible appendix labels, plain-language methods, rounding notes, historical coverage and reconciliation all improved.

The methodology initially buried the useful explanation in internal paths and column mappings. Adding “Methods in brief” helped. The package remained text and data, so I could assess the narrative and numbers but not actual chart legibility or slide layout. The Q2 table and twenty-country Q1 chart still need visual attention during PowerPoint assembly.

Release was the smoothest final step. The agent gave the exact destination, confirmed the copy matched, listed the contents and explained that later changes require release 002. I appreciated the clear separation between revisable draft and frozen release.

## 7. Wasted turns or time

The biggest avoidable delay was needing to ask for drafting more than once before receiving files. Routine commit approval, staged method questions and a separate decision on exporting a small reconciliation table also consumed attention unnecessarily.

The agent reported four review rounds before the first draft was clean. I cannot assess the time or internals of those rounds, but the remaining substantive errors made the emphasis on clean reviews less reassuring. I would rather receive one concise account of what was verified and what still needs my judgment.

Correcting the trade wording, documenting historical gaps and resolving the reconciliation were worthwhile uses of review time. They should ideally have been caught before the draft reached me.
